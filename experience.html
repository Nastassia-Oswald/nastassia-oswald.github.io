Workshop

Turning research silos into discovery systems

3 hours · online or onsite · 4 to 8 people or a cross-functional group

A hands-on workshop for teams who want to line up around their insights and make discovery continuous.

Why it matters

Everyone is learning, nobody is connecting it

Most teams collect insights in isolation. Marketing runs customer calls, sales hears the objections, product and design run usability tests. Each team builds useful knowledge, but it rarely connects, so you get noise instead of a shared picture.

This workshop turns that scattered discovery into one system that every team adds to and learns from.

What you’ll learn

4 things to take back

Map

Where your insights already come from, and how to connect them.

Gaps

How to make knowledge gaps visible across teams.

Flow

How to get discovery moving both ways between commercial and product.

Tools

Practical frameworks and templates for working together.

Format

3 hours, hands-on

Online or onsite, built around interactive exercises, and tailored to how mature your discovery practice is today. Sessions work for small teams of 4 to 8 people or for larger cross-functional groups.

Outcomes

What you leave with

  • A shared discovery framework and the workshop templates
  • A clear view of where your research silos are
  • Concrete next steps to keep discovery continuous

Who it’s for

Teams that share customers but not insights

  • Product managers and designers
  • Commercial and sales leads
  • Research or operations roles that sit between teams
  • Anyone dealing with scattered insights or priorities that don’t line up

2025 – 2026 · Ness Digital Engineering for S&P Global Mobility

Opening S&P Global’s pricing platform to carmakers in 3 shippable layers, from concept to working backend in 4 months

Turning an analyst’s tool into a product carmakers can use · Enterprise · automotive data · B2B

Role
Senior Strategic Product Designer
When
2025 – 2026
Client
S&P Global Mobility
Product
MSRP vehicle pricing platform

S&P Global’s vehicle pricing platform was built for its own analysts. The goal was to open it up to carmakers.

01 · The problem

2 teams, 2 environments

Volume forecasts lived in one environment and pricing adjustments in another. My job was to define how the 2 teams and their data could work together without blocking each other.

02 · My part

Ship it in layers

I split the work into 3 layers so it could ship in parts: analysis, simulation and workflow. Pricing analysts can start baseline scenarios on their own, and new volume data is pulled into existing scenarios on request instead of being forced in.

03 · Key decision

Clear owners before any code

I set up a RACI between Ness and S&P Global so dependencies such as data ingestion had a clear owner, and a Sprint 0 cleared infrastructure blockers before development started.

Outcome

From concept to a working backend in 4 months

The project went from concept architecture to a working backend phase in 4 months.

2022 – 2025 · Bryter · Principal UX Designer

Prototyping AI agents for compliance teams that turned 30 trials into 5 paying customers and a Product of the Year shortlist

From legal intake to AI agents for compliance teams · Legal tech & compliance AI

Role
Principal UX Designer · discovery & research
When
Jan 2023 – Aug 2024
Team
PM, engineering lead, 4 developers, legal advisor
Domain
Legal, Governance, Risk & Compliance

Bryter's low-code platform was powerful but too heavy for busy lawyers. Over 18 months we tried a ready-made legal intake product, dropped it when buyers showed no urgency, pivoted to AI for compliance teams, launched with paying customers, and then moved back to legal when demand data pointed there.

discovery calls for legal intake
30
trial customers from prototype-led calls
5

WIP

Work in progress. I’m still updating this case study, so some parts are shorter than they will be.

Recognition

Finalist, Product of the Year

Bryter was shortlisted for Product of the Year at the ALM Law.com European Legal Innovation & Technology Awards 2025, held in Milan.

01 · Research changed my mind

An intake product nobody was pulling for

We started with legal intake and matter management. I believed matter management and triage were the value. Our sandbox customers, Allianz, Bosch, Solaris and TD Synnex, ignored both after a few weeks; they only leaned in for the automation. We stripped the product back to intake alone.

Buyers had budget but no urgency. Meanwhile companies were moving money into AI, often without a use case, and the CEO’s strategy team picked Governance, Risk and Compliance, with company policies as the way in.

Nobody knew whether that market was real.

My job: find out quickly, without building much.

02 · My part

Every call tracked, then mapped

Every prospect session went into one funnel, in the prospect’s own words. Insights became candidate features, each tagged with the prospects it would serve. That gave the strategy team 3 regulated areas to test: OSHA rules for manufacturing, energy and construction; HR; and anti-bribery compliance, which applies to every industry.

03 · My part

The buyer wasn't the user

Compliance bought the product. Everyone else had to use it.

Manufacturing workers

Often no computer at hand. Warnings are printed as signs.

Clinicians

Know their protocols. No time to wait for GRC.

Desk workers

Can look the policy up themselves.

Expert users

GRC and HR: keep policies current, train, report.

04 · Screens

Two looks for the first prototype

The CEO wanted something distinct from the platform, purple instead of blue, before any brand existed. I went colourful and playful first, against the grey platform; the team loved it. Leadership wanted it more serious, so version 2 is calmer. The scope stayed at 4 features: upload, chat with those documents, references, feedback. Plus one fake page, a mocked analytics view, to see what prospects reached for.

05 · Key decision

We sat in the calls ourselves

Once the prototype existed, the PM and I joined prospect sessions instead of reading the notes afterwards. Notes tell you what was said; being in the room tells you what mattered. It confirmed something I now always push for: when the people building hear prospects first-hand, ownership goes up and the build moves faster.

06 · Screens

Every answer shows where it came from

Answers come only from the policies the company uploaded, never the open web. Every answer points to the passage it came from, highlighted, and every answer can be marked wrong. In a regulated space, that is what made people trust it.

07 · Screens

The fake page that became the product

The mocked insights on the first dashboard, contradictions, legibility and best practice, got the strongest reactions in calls. So they became the real Policy Agent overview. Cheap test first, then build.

08 · Key decision

Rebuilt around who can see what

We kept the trial customers close, watched how they used the product, and reworked the information architecture around roles: admins, compliance teams, experts and employees. Soft launch came in April 2024, before we had a brand, with 5 paying customers: Bertelsmann, Zimmer Biomet, Essity, TD Synnex and Datasite.

09 · Screens

Designing AI Agents with 1 developer

Policy AI became AI Agents, each one specialised in a single regulation. Commercial wanted agents in their own space, and we had 1 frontend developer. I explored options inside the existing UI and added one menu layer. It looked like a lot on paper; users got it straight away.

10 · Screens

Back to legal: the Contract Agent

When demand pointed back to legal teams, the patterns carried over: projects for each review, chat grounded in the documents you attach, and extracted fields you can check against the source.

What I'd do differently

We had the technology before the market

Legal: loud

Constant media coverage. New AI tools every week.

Compliance: quiet

Our SEO showed no AI search traffic. Sales handovers showed flat interest.

Look for demand signals before the build, not after it. We also lost the argument about feature names: users wanted "Chat", commercial wanted something that sounded sophisticated. I collected customer quotes to make the case.

01 · The problem

March 2020: the trips stopped

Before

Babbel Travel sold language trips to schools abroad.

March 2020

Travel stopped, and the product with it.

The real problem

Learners had no way to get close to the language. Alone, lessons felt repetitive and people dropped off.

So: how do we bring the country to them?

02 · My part

A 3-month plan to find out, cheaply

Question it

Mythbusters on our assumptions. Research objectives. Should progress-seekers come first?

Test it

Landing page smoke test, pop-up surveys in the product, customer support validating ideas.

Build it with people

Learners who had booked a trip, co-creation with language schools, virtual events and workshops.

03 · My part

How the research was run

8 in-depth interviews: 4 users and 4 non-users, 4 beginners and 4 intermediates, in English and Spanish. Two prompts did most of the work: “When you think of culture, what comes to mind?”, answered in words and then pictures, and “A foreign friend wants to learn your culture. Where should they start?”

04 · Key decision

One hypothesis, 5 cheap concepts

Before any concept, we agreed one hypothesis: closeness to the culture is what makes reviewing worth doing.

ConceptWhat testing taught usWhere it landed

05 · Key decision

We killed Culture Classroom

Cooking, language and live interaction at once was cognitive overload, and live cooking classes were far from our strengths and hard to scale. But learners really did want language and cooking together, so what survived became 2 cheaper ideas: culinary videos and the events marketplace.

06 · Key decision

Tested where learners already were

A guided food walk through Rome went out on Babbel Travel’s social channels. Passport To, audio stories from locals with quizzes, started with Edinburgh. I also worked with the game studio on in-app mini-games and connected every cultural experience back to what learners had studied, so it doubled as review.

Outcome

An audio adventure the Webbys noticed

The interactive audio story, Aventura on the Babbel Express, was named an Honoree in the 2024 Webby Awards, Science and Education Podcast category. The casual learning experiences got clearer goals, progress carried over from lessons, and tracking from day one.

What I'd do differently

What you count is what you'll build

What Learner Minutes told us

People were spending time in the cultural experiences.

What it never told us

Whether they were progressing.

2019 – 2020 · Körber Digital · Product Manager / Senior Strategic Designer

Shadowing cell and gene therapy teams for 5 days to frame a supply chain product that moved forward as a prototype

SafeCell · A supply chain where one lost ID can cost a patient their therapy · healthcare, pharma, logistics

Role

Team

Cell and gene therapies treat blood cancers and genetic diseases by taking a patient's own cells, altering them, and giving them back. SafeCell was a digital, end-to-end supply chain for that process, from admitting the patient to the cure.

4

2

1

01 · The problem

Phone calls, emails and paper forms

Clinics, blood collection centres, couriers and manufacturers coordinated each therapy by email and phone, each with a different identifier for the same patient and its own paper forms. With few patients this worked. As demand grew, it would not scale, and in this chain a mix-up is not an inconvenience.

02 · My part

Assumptions first, then fieldwork

Before any research, we wrote down every assumption the team held and ranked them by how much the project depended on each. That list steered 5 days of shadowing at collection centres with the Blood Centers of America, watching nurses at work and interviewing oncologists and nurses.

03 · Synthesis

The pattern showed up on the wall

We transcribed the interviews, tagged quotes and clustered them. The simplest tool helped most: a wall of sticky notes, where it became clear that every party struggled with the same 2 things.

04 · Outcome

Orchestration and compliance, at scale

For every player in the chain, orchestration and compliance were the big problems. SafeCell was framed as the least disruptive way to solve both while keeping quality at scale, and taken forward as a prototype in Körber’s innovation funnel.

01 · The problem

Years of fast growth, years of debt

A product study showed years of usability problems and technical debt across mobile and desktop web. The hard part was the size of it: finding, documenting and redesigning every flow variation and edge case, while 4 cross-functional teams delivered in parallel.

02 · My part

1-week prototype cycles

We started with interviews with users and people in our target group, then ran 1-week cycles of quick prototypes and user feedback. Once the big themes were clear, developers started on the large changes while we refined the details. Nov 2017 to Aug 2018.

03 · What we learnt

Research on the phone, booking in peace

People use short moments on their phone for first research, and often share the trip with the person they travel with. When it comes to booking, they want a calm moment, and the device they pick depends on how complex the journey is. So the flow had to adapt to the device, not the other way round.

04 · Multi-modal

Booking a multi-modal journey in one go

A missed flight is the costly part of a trip, so people buy flights first and fit trains and buses around them later. The goal was a new flow to book a complex journey in one go, with several legs and modes of transport. Aug to Oct 2018.

Outcome

Flexibility around the flight

Flexibility before and after the flight mattered most to people, because that’s the least predictable part of the trip. Giving them options to adjust took away a lot of the anxiety. Research up front made the design more confident and meant far fewer iterations after launch.
